Section I: Bezier Curves and Surfaces

Part 1: Bezier curves with 1D de Casteljau subdivision

De Casteljau's algorithm is a formula that interpolates between control points to generate points that on a curve that connects the start and end points. Based on a distance t in [0,1], we use the formula \( (1-t)(p_1) + (t)(p_2) \) between all adjacent control points \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) and return the list of resulting sums as the intermediate points. This method is used and repeated until only one sum remains, which is the resulting point that lies on the bezier curve at parameter t, where t=0 indicates the starting point and t=1 indicates the ending point.

Part 2: Bezier surfaces with separable 1D de Casteljau

Similarly, evaluating a Bézier surface follows the same principle as evaluating a Bézier curve, except that it uses two parameters, \(u\) and \(v\), to specify a point across the two dimensions of the surface. We first evaluate the Bézier curve along each row of 3D control points using the parameter \(u\). At each step, we interpolate between adjacent control points \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) using the formula \((1-u)p_1 + up_2\), repeating this process until each row produces a single 3D point. We then use the parameter \(v\) to evaluate the Bézier curve through the resulting points, interpolating until only one point remains. The final 3D point lies on the Bézier surface at parameters \((u,v)\).

Section II: Triangle Meshes and Half-Edge Data Structure

Part 3: Area-weighted vertex normals

To calculate the unit normal at a vertex, we compute the area-weighted average of the normals of the faces incident to that vertex, then normalize the resulting vector. I adapted the traversal in the printNeighbors() method to iterate through the faces adjacent to the vertex. For each face, I retrieve the other two vertices, \(v_1\) and \(v_2\), and compute the cross product of their position vectors relative to the current vertex: cross((v1 − this->position),(v2−this->position)) Which results in a vector perpendicular to the face with a magnitude proportional to twice the triangle's area. After adding all cross products across all incident faces, I normalize the sum using unit() to obtain the vertex's unit normal.

Part 4: Edge flip

To implement the edge flipping operation, it was extremely helpful to draw out the operation on paper before implementing it in C++. This way, I could keep track of the pointers that needed to be changed and which ones needed to stay the same. I began by saving references to the existing halfedges, vertices, faces, and edge so that I could safely update their connectivity. I then changed the vertex assignments of the two halfedges along the flipped edge, updated the next() pointers to establish the new triangular face cycles, and reassigned the face and vertex representative halfedges to maintain a consistent mesh structure. Finally, I ensured that the flipped edge continued to reference the appropriate halfedge. I think I did not have a very eventful debugging journey, largely due to drawing the operation on paper beforehand.

Part 5: Edge split

Similarly, to implement the edge-splitting operation, I found it extremely helpful to draw out the operation on paper before implementing it in C++. I began by saving references to the existing halfedges, vertices, faces, and edges so that I could safely update their connectivity. Next, I created a new vertex m at the midpoint of the original edge's endpoints. I then created three new edges and six new halfedges to connect m to the surrounding vertices, including the two vertices opposite the original edge in its adjacent triangular faces. I also created two new faces, while updating the connectivity of the two existing faces. Finally, I updated the corresponding vertex, edge, face, and next() pointers to establish the four new triangular face cycles and ensure that the mesh maintained consistent connectivity. I think I did not have a very eventful debugging journey throughout part 5, but did have to do some debugging when edge splitting was used in part 6.
